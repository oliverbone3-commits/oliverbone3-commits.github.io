A weather app that research turned into a health tracker

WeatherWell began as a weather app with wellbeing hints. I ran the research behind it, more than 200 hours of it, and it showed that people cared about how they would feel, not about the weather. Over 10–15 iterations we turned it into a health tracker with the weather as context.

Role

Product
WeatherWell, an iOS app on how weather affects the way you feel
Timeline
Nov 2019 – Sep 2021
Scale

How do you want to read the case?

WeatherWell started as a weather app with light wellbeing forecasts. With the CPO I broke “does weather affect health” into three testable questions, wrote the studies and listened to more than 200 hours of UserTesting recordings myself. People didn't care about the weather; they cared about how they would feel and what to do about it. Over 10–15 iterations the product became a health tracker with weather as context, with scales instead of verdicts so it didn't feed anxiety. By the fund's numbers, retention matched the best apps in its portfolio.

01 · The question

“Does weather affect health” is too big to test

  • Product-market fit
  • 3 testable questions
  • With the CPO

We were a four-person founding team looking for product-market fit in a niche with no ready data. The big hypothesis, that weather affects how people feel, couldn't be confirmed or killed in one go. With the CPO I split it into questions a study could answer:

  • Will people log how they feel every day?
  • Do they believe the weather affects them?
  • Do they trust what the app tells them?

02 · Research

200 hours of people using the app, listened to by me

  • UserTesting
  • 200+ hours
  • Transcribed myself

I wrote long questionnaires and ran them on UserTesting, where every answer comes with a screen recording: you see what people say and whether they actually understand the interface. I listened to and transcribed more than 200 hours of recordings myself, and fed the findings into the next iterations.

The finding

People didn't care about the weather. They could check it anywhere. They cared about how they would feel and what to do about it, and it mattered to them that their own sense of “the weather gets to me” was confirmed.

That last part shaped the product: every insight asks “Relevant to you?”, and the outlook learns from each person's own check-ins instead of telling them how they should feel.

03 · Decision

Start with the weather, on purpose

  • Weather app first
  • Soft entry
  • Test the market

The first version was a weather app with radar, hurricane tracking, severe weather warnings, hourly and daily forecasts, light wellbeing hints and doctor-reviewed tips. It was a deliberate first stage, not the goal: we didn't want to open with “we predict how you'll feel” before knowing whether people were ready to believe it.

04 · Decision

From a weather app with health tips to a health tracker with weather

  • 10–15 iterations
  • How you may feel
  • Week plan

Once the studies came back, the product turned around. Health moved to the centre and the weather shrank to a single card of context. Over 10–15 iterations, each one tested again, the home screen kept changing; five of its versions are below.

The team worked in a tight loop. I'd put a concept together in about fifteen minutes, we'd go through it together, change what didn't hold up and send it to testers the same day. Most design decisions came out of that loop, made by the whole team rather than by one person.

In the final version, Today opens with how you may feel (headaches, joints, energy, sleep), each with a likelihood and the reason behind it. Tap a risk and it explains itself: when it peaks and what drives it. Once analytics ran over the check-ins, we could see which weather factors went with which complaints, so pressure swings, humidity and wind show up as drivers with their strength. This week turns the same forecast into a plan: the hard days, the easy one, and what to move.

05 · Decision

Help people feel better without feeding hypochondria

  • Scales, not verdicts
  • Honest notice
  • Stories

A health forecast has a side effect: tell an anxious person they may get a headache, and they may start to feel one. In the tests many people described their symptoms vividly, so this became a problem of its own: inform without adding anxiety, and keep the self-reports clean. What we did:

  • Scales instead of verdicts. People rate how they feel on a scale, and forecasts show a likelihood, not “you will have a headache”.
  • A notice that never goes away. The forecast isn't exact and everyone reacts differently; it gets more accurate the more often you check in. It is a warning and a reason to log at the same time.
  • Stories, not alarms. A risk comes with a short story: why it happens and what helps today, verified by a physician. People get a plan instead of a scare.

06 · Honest part

What I got wrong

  • Compact cards
  • Simplified weather

Less isn’t automatically lighter. Test the amount.

Note to self

Compact cards. Early on I pushed for short insight cards that open on tap: less information should feel lighter. Testing proved me wrong; people simply didn't notice the short cards. Part of it was copy: every weather event needed its own clear wording, and I hadn't yet learned to help the team with that. We moved to fuller cards with the advice visible right away. Since then I test the amount of information instead of assuming less is better, and treat copy as part of the design.

Simplified weather. At the weather stage I proposed showing the forecast in large periods of the day. Research said people wanted a full hourly forecast for the next 24 hours, so that's what we built.

07 · Result

What changed after

  • Retention on par with the best
  • ~10,000 users a month
  • Monetisation ready
Retention among the best

By the fund's numbers, retention was on par with the best apps in its portfolio.

Growth on plan

Without a big ad budget the product kept to its plan of about 10,000 new users a month.

Monetisation built

Paid features were ready to roll out; the board held them back to keep the focus on retention.

Closed in 2022

The fund changed strategy and closed the projects that weren't earning yet. I had left in September 2021.
