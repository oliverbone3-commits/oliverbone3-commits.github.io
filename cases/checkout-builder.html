A checkout builder that grew from blocks into a payment-flow editor

Every payment method, region and provider brand used to get its own hand-drawn form. I turned them into ~30 blocks with two-axis theming and a working prototype that the checkout engineer took into production. Then it grew into a flow editor: the whole payment scenario as a graph, with every screen assembled from the same blocks.

How do you want to read the case?

Every new payment method or region was drawn from scratch, and light provider brands broke contrast. I turned dozens of forms into ~30 blocks with two-axis theming, then built a working prototype for the business and rebuilt its structure in a day once it became a pile of features. 01 · The problem

Every "add a region" task started almost from zero

  • New region = new drawing
  • 4 fonts in files
  • Hard-coded colours

Launching a new region or payment method meant waiting for a designer to draw its checkout, almost from scratch. A payment gateway shows the buyer a checkout: card, bank transfer, QR, mobile money, local wallets. Every method, region and provider brand needed its own form. They were drawn screen by screen: four fonts in the files, hard-coded colours, and the same blocks — amount, timer, copyable details, instructions — redrawn with small differences each time.

Second attempt

I first proposed a checkout builder at Payler, my previous company. Engineering was overloaded and it never got capacity. At my next company the same pain came back, and this time I built it.

02 · Decision

Blocks, not screens

  • ~12 regions reviewed
  • ~30 blocks
  • Fix once, everywhere

I went through the payment methods of about twelve regions and pulled out what repeats. The result was ~30 blocks with properties instead of a library of finished screens:

  • Basic: button, input, toggle, badge, timer, language switch.
  • Payment: amount header, copy field, bank row, QR, bank and app lists, operator choice, step-by-step instructions.
  • Service: notices, waiting and result states, confirmation dialogs.

A new region became an assembly task. A screen is built from instances, so a fix in one block reaches every checkout. Same rule as in the design system audit: fix the source, not the copies.

03 · Decision

Theme is two axes, not twenty modes

  • Light / dark
  • Brand
  • 1 anchor colour per brand

Ten brands times light and dark would have meant twenty theme modes and hit Figma's plan limit. I split theming into two independent axes: light/dark and brand. Each brand scale is generated from one anchor colour, so hover, pressed and tints come from a formula, not from hand-picking.

Rejected

One mode per brand and theme: 20 modes, every new brand adds two more, plan limit reached.

Chosen

Brand × light/dark as separate axes. A new brand is one anchor colour; the rest is generated.

The hard part was light brands — yellow, light blue — where white text fails. Black text looked foreign on them. The fix was a brand-dependent "text on brand" token: a deep shade of the brand colour itself, with contrast of at least 5.5:1.

Mobile got its own axis too. At 320 px the desktop paddings ate 27.5% of the width, so viewport spacing became a separate collection, independent of theme.

04 · Decision

Show the business a product, not mockups

  • Prototype in one HTML file
  • 5 iterations in a day
  • CSS variables + JSON tokens

People who decide on a merchant-facing builder don't open Figma. So I built a working prototype in one HTML file: the live checkout on the right, settings on the left, export of CSS variables and JSON tokens.

By the third iteration it had turned into a pile of features: fifteen equal sections in one flat list. I stopped and researched how leading payment providers and e-commerce platforms let merchants configure checkouts, then audited the prototype against it. Three findings changed the structure:

  • there are three different jobs: one-time setup, variants per market, and rare compliance edits;
  • the "field state preview" was a testing tool pretending to be a setting;
  • the country preset felt like a restriction, though it was really a quick-fill.

Version 4 got a new structure: side navigation with six sections, state preview moved to a separate dev toolbar, the compliance block locked, and no way to switch off the last payment method. Version 5 added a dashboard of named configurations, which turned the builder into a product with objects rather than a panel of sliders. Five iterations took one day.

05 · Honest part

What went wrong

  • Features before jobs
  • Token layers beyond the team
  • Brief read literally

Ask who sets it up, why and how often — before adding the option.

Note to self
  • Features before jobs. Three iterations added options without asking who configures this, why and how often. Researching the jobs first would have saved them.
  • Token architecture beyond the team. I rebuilt the builder on an open UI kit with three token layers: 145 primitives and 35 semantic tokens. It was correct and too complex for daily work. Together with the team we went back to one collection of 43 tokens that people actually use.
  • Reading the brief literally. One checkout had to be redone: "manual entry" meant input fields, not copyable details.

07 · Where it went

From a settings panel to an editor for the whole payment flow

  • Flows list with checks
  • Graph of screens and operations
  • Backend contract
  • Preview as a payer
  • 19 components

A checkout isn't one screen. It is a scenario: choose a method, pay, wait for the bank, succeed or fail, retry, cancel, time out. The settings panel could style the screens but not the scenario between them. I went through the prototype with the checkout engineer; he gathered every checkout we had and extended it to cover them, and I redesigned his second iteration into a flow editor.

Every flow, with its problems in plain sight

The flows list shows each checkout flow with its entry screen, number of screens and checks: No issues, 2 warnings, 8 errors · 4 warnings. A broken flow is visible before anyone opens it.

A checkout as a graph

In Flow mode the scenario is a canvas. Screens are nodes with the actions a payer can take: pay, cancel, report. Between them sit backend operations (submit, status, cancel), and every outcome an operation can return (success, pending, failure, default) is its own exit that has to lead somewhere. Events that can happen anywhere, like cancel and timeout, hang off a single From any screen node instead of being wired to every screen. A pending screen polls status on its own. The same graph can be read as a flowchart or re-laid out as a graph.

The backend contract on the same canvas

The inspector has three tabs: Screen, Transition, Operations. Operations is the contract with the backend: what we call, which fields go in, which outcomes may come back, and which values land in {{placeholders}} on the screens. Designers, frontend and backend are meant to look at one source instead of a spec and a mockup that drift apart.

Screens from the same blocks

In Form mode a screen is a shared header (amount, language switcher, timer, payment ID), a body of steps and a footer of buttons, assembled from a palette of ready-made blocks and fields: card, card without CVV, phone, text input, copyable value, details card. One flow can serve several methods, filtered as All methods, Card or UPI. Texts are kept per language, and the theme keeps the two axes from the block system.

Run it as a payer, hand it off as code

Preview clicks through the whole flow as a payer, with backend calls simulated, so a broken branch would show up before release. The Component catalog lists the 19 form components with their props as JSON, ready to copy into the frontend.
