How do you want to read the case?

At CoinsPaid, a crypto payment processor, I was the only product designer while the company grew from 13 to about 160 people. I designed the first processing platform and the consumer wallet, and ran the wallet as its product owner: market analysis, the app, the tracking plan in Amplitude. The wallet was built around an address book: you send to Satoshi, not to 34 characters, and keep separate receiving addresses for separate payers. It was frozen when the company chose to stay B2B. I also made the CoinsPaid brand, whose symbol is still in use, and replaced an outside agency with designers I hired.

01 · Context

A crypto processor growing faster than its interfaces

  • Payment gateway
  • Business wallet
  • Exchange
  • 13 → ~160 people

CoinsPaid is a crypto payment processor founded in 2014 in Tallinn. Businesses use it to accept and pay out crypto: a payment gateway, a business wallet, exchange and the hot wallets behind them. While I was there the company grew from 13 to about 160 people, and every new product and every new team needed screens. I was the only product designer, so the work was always two things at once: ship the next interface and keep the ones already shipped consistent.

02 · Platform

The first processing platform

  • First iterations
  • Grew with the company

I designed the first iterations of the processing platform, the part businesses use to accept and pay out crypto. They stayed in use for more than two years while the company grew around them, and every new team that appeared needed its screens from the same single designer.

03 · The wallet

The wallet, as designer and product owner

  • Market analysis
  • Address book
  • Metrics and tracking

On the wallet I was both the designer and the mobile product owner: I ran the market analysis, designed the app and introduced the metrics it was judged by. None of the wallets I reviewed at the time had an address book (today most do), so I made it the core of the app: sending crypto to a pasted address is where people make expensive mistakes. I also wrote the Amplitude tracking plan: every event, where it fires and what question it answers.

The App Store page sold it on five things: free and instant payments between CoinsPaid users, quick exchange, several addresses for one wallet, sending crypto to friends by name, and all balances in one place.

04 · The app

Send to a name, keep addresses apart

  • Contacts
  • My addresses
  • Exchange

Two ideas carried the app. Contacts: you send to Satoshi, not to 34 characters, and every contact keeps the history of what went between you. My addresses: one wallet, several receiving addresses with names like Freelance or Secret Project, so the people who pay you don't see each other on the blockchain. Exchange held the rate while you confirmed, with MIN, HALF and MAX next to the keypad.

05 · In the browser

The same wallet on a laptop

  • Side panel
  • Currency first
  • Address check

The wallet also ran in a browser, on the same API. On a wide screen the balances stay in view: a balance, a contact or a transaction opens in a side panel with its actions on top. Sending starts with the currency, then shows only the contacts who have an address for it, and an address pasted for the wrong network is stopped before anything leaves the wallet.

06 · Frozen

Why the wallet stopped

  • Same rails as B2B
  • No biometrics
  • Frozen

A consumer wallet needs its own team, not the B2B rails.

Note to self

The wallet ran on the same rails and API as the processing business: a web app without biometrics. It couldn't compete as a consumer product, and I argued it needed its own team and backend. The company froze it as a non-priority, which was a fair call for a B2B business.

07 · Brand

A brand and a symbol still in use, and a team to carry them

  • Logo mark
  • In-house designers
  • Paid test tasks

I developed the CoinsPaid branding and rolled it out, including the symbol that is still in use. When the product side was covered, I moved to marketing for a while: replaced an expensive outside agency with in-house graphic designers, hired through paid test tasks, and set the visual style they worked in.
